ⓘ approach

Every star feels one fixed “field”: the galaxy's gravitational potential, built from smooth components (thin + thick disk, bulge, halo) plus rigidly rotating m=2 waves — a logarithmic spiral (the calm default) and an optional bar. Stars are integrated on their own orbits with a symplectic leapfrog; there are no star–star forces and no friction. Toggling a mode fades its amplitude over a few time units, so experiments never jolt the disk. When bar and spiral share one pattern speed the response never beats; detune Ωs with the bar on and the HUD warns that beating washes the arms out.

The spiral arms are not material — they are the rotating crests of the field where stars bunch up as they pass, so the pattern never winds up while each star streams through it. The response is steadiest between the inner resonance and corotation (marked rings); beyond corotation the disk reads smoother and stars disperse — that outer scatter is real orbit physics, not noise. As the settle-up window ends — the moment the live view begins — the warm disk is seeded pre-aligned with the wave (each orbit's apocenter placed in the potential trough along the same winding law — where the forced response actually crests; the outer disk from corotation outward is seeded here, not at t=0), so full arms are visible immediately and the seed's own winding transient is born inside the capture window; that kinematic alignment then shears away over tens of time units while the wave's forcing keeps the inner arms locked. The cold young disk (blue) responds most sharply, tracing crisp arms like the star-forming ridges of real galaxies, while the warmer old disk stays smooth underneath. The bar (when on) works the same way: inside corotation its field captures stars onto elongated resonant (x1) orbits; at resonances stars trade angular momentum with the pattern and migrate — outward into rings, or onto eccentric inclined orbits that puff into the halo belt. That is how disk turns into bar and belt without spawning anything.

The soft glow under the stars is a surface-brightness estimator, not a force: disk stars binned, blurred, and filtered to show only the non-axisymmetric excess — the unresolved light a telescope would blend into luminous arms.

The optional “live m=2 (S5)” mode closes a feedback loop: the disk's own m=2 pattern is measured in the pattern frame every substep and added back as a small local self-gravity term (a WKB estimate, 2πGΣ₂/k, low-passed and capped). It is off by default and deliberately so — measured honestly, it strengthens the steady arms inside corotation by about a tenth and extends the settle transient in the outer disk, but it cannot hold arms past R≈4: out there the disk's own response has no coherent moment left to amplify. That negative result is the point; the study and its numbers are in archive/0.4.0-worklog.md. The third select option, “global” (0.5.0), swaps that local estimate for a global m=2 Green's-function solve on the same measured grid: the potential at each radius is fed by the whole disk's response, not just the local bin — at R=4.5 the kernel sees ~12% of the imposed spiral field where the WKB line saw 0.1%. galaxy.js itself is untouched; the variant lives in selfgrav.js and is gated by check-poisson / check-selfgrav-phase before any closed-loop claim.

Because the system is Hamiltonian — dissipation free, symplectically integrated — nothing decays, nothing clumps; the same stars are there forever. (Optional “drag” toggle shows the opposite: pure friction piles stars into the center — why friction is the wrong tool here.)
